1 What Are Life Processes?
Life Processes are the basic maintenance functions that all living organisms must keep performing in order to stay alive — even when they are not doing anything visible. They keep the organised, cell-based structure of the body from breaking down.

The four life processes covered in this chapter are:

Life ProcessWhat it does
NutritionTaking in food (raw materials + energy source) and using it for growth and repair
RespirationBreaking down food to release usable energy in the form of ATP
TransportationMoving food, oxygen, CO₂ and waste to/from every cell of the body
ExcretionRemoving harmful metabolic waste products from the body
Why molecular movement = life Even when an organism looks perfectly still, molecules inside its cells are constantly moving and being repaired/replaced. This invisible molecular movement is why even a sleeping animal or a non-growing plant is still considered alive, while a virus (which shows no molecular movement until it infects a cell) is debated as "alive or not."
2 Nutrition
Nutrition is the process by which an organism obtains and utilises food to get energy and raw materials for growth, development and repair.
2.1 Autotrophic Nutrition (self-made food)

Autotrophs (green plants, some bacteria) make their own food from simple inorganic substances — CO₂ and water — using an external energy source (sunlight) and chlorophyll. This process is called photosynthesis.

Photosynthesis Reaction
6CO₂ + 6H₂O  (Sunlight, Chlorophyll)  →  C₆H₁₂O₆ (Glucose) + 6O₂

Three steps of photosynthesis:
  1. Absorption of light energy by chlorophyll
  2. Conversion of light energy to chemical energy and splitting of water into hydrogen and oxygen
  3. Reduction of CO₂ to carbohydrates

Raw materials for photosynthesis:

  • CO₂ — enters through stomata (tiny pores on leaf surface)
  • Water — absorbed from soil by roots
  • Chlorophyll — green pigment in chloroplasts
  • Sunlight — the energy source

Other raw materials from soil:

  • Nitrogen — needed for making proteins (taken up as nitrates/nitrites, or via nitrogen-fixing bacteria)
  • Phosphorus, iron, magnesium — other essential minerals
Activity Recall The starch test (using iodine solution, which turns blue-black in the presence of starch) is used to prove that (i) sunlight, (ii) chlorophyll and (iii) CO₂ are all essential for photosynthesis. A destarched, variegated leaf is used — only the green, illuminated parts of the leaf turn blue-black with iodine.
2.2 Heterotrophic Nutrition

Heterotrophs cannot make their own food; they depend directly or indirectly on autotrophs. There are three main strategies:

TypeHow food is obtainedExamples
SaprotrophicBreaks down food outside the body, then absorbs itBread mould, yeast, mushroom
ParasiticDerives nutrition from another living organism without killing itCuscuta (amar-bel), ticks, lice, leeches, tapeworm
HolozoicTakes in whole/solid food and digests it internallyAmoeba, most animals, humans

The alimentary canal is a long tube from the mouth to the anus, with different regions specialised for different functions.

3 Respiration
Respiration is the process of breaking down food (glucose) inside cells to release energy, stored in the form of ATP (adenosine triphosphate) — the "energy currency" used to drive all other cellular reactions.

Step 1 (always the same): Glucose (6-carbon) is broken down in the cytoplasm into pyruvate (3-carbon). What happens next depends on the organism and on oxygen availability:

Point of differenceAerobic RespirationAnaerobic Respiration
Oxygen required?YesNo
Where it occursCytoplasm + MitochondriaCytoplasm only
End productsCO₂ + H₂OEthanol + CO₂ (yeast) OR lactic acid (muscles)
Energy releasedMuch moreMuch less
Breathing ≠ Respiration Breathing is only the physical process of taking air in and out of lungs (inhalation/exhalation). Respiration is the chemical, cellular process of breaking down glucose to release energy. Breathing is just one part that supports respiration — it is NOT the same thing.
3.1 The Human Respiratory System

📖 See NCERT textbook Fig. 6.9 (Human respiratory system) — draw & label it in your notebook exactly as shown there.

Mechanism of breathing: Ribs lift up and diaphragm flattens → chest cavity becomes larger → air is sucked into the lungs and fills the alveoli. Blood in alveolar capillaries releases CO₂ into the alveoli and picks up O₂.

Why alveoli are so efficient Alveoli are tiny, balloon-like sacs with a very thin wall and are surrounded by an extensive network of blood capillaries — this gives an enormous surface area for fast, efficient exchange of O₂ and CO₂. (Estimated alveolar surface in an adult ≈ 80 m²!)
3.2 Respiratory Pigment & Gas Transport

In large-bodied animals, simple diffusion is not enough to deliver oxygen to every cell, so a respiratory pigment is used. In humans this is haemoglobin, present in red blood cells, which has a very high affinity for oxygen. CO₂ is more soluble in water than O₂, so it is mostly transported in a dissolved form in blood plasma.

Terrestrial vs Aquatic organisms:

  • Terrestrial organisms breathe atmospheric O₂ (about 21% O₂ in air) — easier to get.
  • Aquatic organisms use O₂ dissolved in water (much lower amount) — so aquatic animals like fish breathe much faster than terrestrial animals to get enough O₂.

4 Transportation
4.1 Transportation in Human Beings

Blood is a fluid connective tissue made of plasma (fluid medium that transports food, CO₂ and nitrogenous waste in dissolved form) in which cells are suspended. O₂ is carried mainly by red blood cells (using haemoglobin).

★ MUST-DRAW DIAGRAM (Board Annexure): Structural view of the Human Heart — label: right/left atrium, right/left ventricle, vena cava, pulmonary artery, pulmonary vein, aorta, septum.
📖 See NCERT textbook Fig. 6.10 (Sectional view of the human heart) — draw & label it in your notebook exactly as shown there.

How the heart pumps (double circulation):

  1. Oxygen-rich blood from lungs enters the left atrium → relaxes & collects blood.
  2. Left atrium contracts → blood moves to left ventricle.
  3. Left ventricle contracts (thick muscular wall) → blood pumped out to the whole body via the aorta.
  4. Deoxygenated blood from body enters right atrium → passes to right ventricle.
  5. Right ventricle pumps blood to the lungs for oxygenation via the pulmonary artery.

Why separate right & left sides? Keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood from mixing → highly efficient O₂ supply, needed by warm-blooded/high-energy animals (birds, mammals). Amphibians/reptiles have 3-chambered hearts and tolerate some mixing; fish have a 2-chambered heart (single circulation).

Blood Vessels
VesselCarries bloodWall thicknessValves?
ArteriesAway from the heart (high pressure)Thick, elasticNo
VeinsTowards the heart (low pressure)ThinYes — prevent backflow
CapillariesConnect arteries to veins in tissuesOne-cell thickNo — allows exchange of material
Blood pressure The force blood exerts against vessel walls. Systolic pressure (ventricles contracting) is normally 120 mm Hg; diastolic pressure (ventricles relaxing) is normally 80 mm Hg. Measured using a sphygmomanometer. High BP (hypertension) can damage arteries and cause internal bleeding.

Platelets: Help form a clot at the site of an injury, plugging the leak and preventing excess blood loss.

Lymph: A tissue fluid similar to plasma (colourless, less protein) that seeps out of capillaries into intercellular spaces, drains into lymphatic capillaries and vessels, and is returned to the blood. It also transports digested & absorbed fat from the intestine, and helps fight infection.

4.2 Transportation in Plants

Plants have two separate transport tissues:

TissueTransportsDirectionDriving mechanism
XylemWater & mineralsRoots → upward to leavesRoot pressure (at night) + Transpiration pull (during day)
PhloemFood (sucrose) & other substancesBoth up & down (from leaves to storage/growing organs)Uses ATP energy (active transport) — called translocation
📖 Refer to your NCERT textbook — Fig. 6.12 (Movement of water vapour during transpiration) for this diagram.
Transpiration Loss of water vapour from aerial parts of the plant (mainly through stomata). It creates a suction (pull) that draws water up from the xylem in roots, helping in (i) upward movement of water & minerals, and (ii) temperature regulation of the plant.
5 Excretion
Excretion is the biological process of removing harmful, nitrogenous metabolic waste products (such as urea and uric acid) from the body.
5.1 Excretion in Human Beings

The human excretory system consists of a pair of kidneys, a pair of ureters, a urinary bladder, and a urethra.

★ MUST-DRAW DIAGRAM (Board Annexure): Human Excretory System — label: kidney, ureter, urinary bladder, urethra, renal artery, renal vein.
📖 See NCERT textbook Fig. 6.13 (Excretory system in human beings) and Fig. 6.14 (Structure of a nephron) — draw & label both in your notebook exactly as shown there.
How urine is formed — the nephron
  1. Filtration: Blood enters the glomerulus (a cluster of very thin-walled capillaries) at high pressure. Small molecules (glucose, amino acids, salts, water, urea) are filtered into the Bowman's capsule.
  2. Selective re-absorption: As the filtrate flows along the tubule, useful substances (glucose, amino acids, most water) are re-absorbed back into the blood.
  3. Urine formation: The remaining filtrate (urea, excess water, salts) becomes urine, which passes into the ureter and is stored in the urinary bladder before release via the urethra.
Did you know? — Dialysis If kidneys fail, an artificial kidney (haemodialysis) can filter nitrogenous wastes from the blood using tubes with a semi-permeable lining suspended in dialysing fluid (same osmotic pressure as blood but no urea). Waste diffuses out of the blood into the fluid and purified blood is pumped back into the patient.
5.2 Excretion in Plants

Plants use very different strategies for excretion — there is no dedicated excretory organ or tissue:

  • O₂ itself is a waste product of photosynthesis; excess water is lost by transpiration.
  • Many waste products are stored in cellular vacuoles, or in leaves that eventually fall off.
  • Some wastes are stored as resins and gums, especially in old xylem tissue.
  • Some waste substances are excreted into the soil around the roots.

Section B — Short Answer Type-I
A13. Aerobic: occurs in the presence of O₂, in cytoplasm + mitochondria, gives CO₂+H₂O, releases much more energy. Anaerobic: occurs without O₂, only in cytoplasm, gives ethanol+CO₂ (or lactic acid), releases much less energy. (Any two contrasting points)
A14. (i) Kills bacteria/germs present in food (ii) Creates an acidic medium that is necessary for the enzyme pepsin to act on proteins.
A15. Herbivores eat grass/plant material (cellulose) which is harder to digest and takes longer, so a longer small intestine is needed to allow complete digestion; carnivores eat meat which is digested faster and need a shorter intestine.
A16. Xylem transports water & minerals only upward (roots→leaves) using root pressure/transpiration pull; Phloem transports food (prepared by photosynthesis) in both upward & downward directions using ATP energy (translocation).
A17. A four-chambered heart completely separates oxygenated and deoxygenated blood (via the septum), giving a highly efficient supply of oxygen needed to maintain a constant high body temperature and energy needs; a two-chambered fish heart allows only single circulation and lower efficiency, sufficient for a cold-blooded aquatic lifestyle.
A18. Transpiration is the loss of water in the form of vapour from the aerial (overground) parts of a plant, mainly through stomata. Functions: (i) helps in upward movement/absorption of water and minerals from roots to leaves (transpiration pull), (ii) helps in temperature regulation of the plant (any one).
A19. Guard cells regulate opening/closing of the stomatal pore. When water flows into guard cells, they swell (become turgid) and the pore opens; when they lose water and shrink (flaccid), the pore closes.
A20. (i) Absorption of light energy by chlorophyll (ii) Conversion of light energy to chemical energy and splitting of water into hydrogen and oxygen (iii) Reduction of carbon dioxide to carbohydrates.
A21. The small intestine has finger-like projections called villi which greatly increase the surface area for absorption, and villi are richly supplied with blood vessels to carry away absorbed food; the stomach lacks such structures and mainly performs digestion, not absorption.
A22. Blood is coloured (red, due to haemoglobin) and contains more protein and blood cells; lymph is colourless and contains less protein and fewer cells. Blood flows only in blood vessels; lymph flows in a separate lymphatic system and also drains excess tissue fluid back into blood. (Any two)
Section C — Short Answer Type-II
A23. Oxygenated blood from the lungs enters the left atrium, which relaxes while collecting blood, then contracts to push blood into the left ventricle. The left ventricle contracts strongly and pumps this oxygenated blood to the whole body via the aorta. Deoxygenated blood from the body enters the right atrium, which contracts to send it to the right ventricle; the right ventricle then pumps it to the lungs for oxygenation via the pulmonary artery. Valves prevent backflow of blood.
A24. (i) Alveoli have very thin walls (one cell thick) allowing fast diffusion of gases. (ii) They are balloon-like, providing an enormous surface area (about 80 m²) for exchange. (iii) They are surrounded by an extensive network of blood capillaries, ensuring blood is always close to the exchange surface.
A25. Amoeba puts out temporary finger-like extensions of its cell surface called pseudopodia, which surround and engulf a food particle. These fuse to form a food vacuole. Inside the food vacuole, complex substances are broken down into simpler ones which diffuse into the cytoplasm; the remaining undigested material is moved to the surface and thrown out. (Diagram: refer NCERT textbook Fig. 6.5 – award marks for labelled stages showing pseudopodia and food vacuole.)
A26. A nephron has a cup-shaped Bowman's capsule surrounding a cluster of thin-walled capillaries called the glomerulus, connected to a long coiled tubule. Blood entering the glomerulus is filtered under pressure into the Bowman's capsule (filtration) — small molecules like glucose, amino acids, salts, and water pass through, along with urea. As filtrate flows along the tubule, useful substances (glucose, amino acids, most water) are selectively re-absorbed into blood. The remaining fluid (urea + excess water/salts) becomes urine and drains into the collecting duct → ureter.
A27. Separating oxygenated and deoxygenated blood allows a highly efficient supply of oxygen to the body, needed by organisms with high energy requirements (to maintain constant body temperature). Two-chambered heart: Fish. Three-chambered heart: Amphibians/Reptiles (e.g. frog). Four-chambered heart: Humans/birds/mammals.
A28. Glucose (6-carbon) is first broken down to pyruvate (3-carbon) in the cytoplasm in all cases. In the presence of oxygen (aerobic respiration, e.g. in human muscle cells during normal activity/mitochondria), pyruvate is broken down completely into CO₂ and water, releasing a large amount of energy. In the absence of oxygen, e.g. in yeast during fermentation (anaerobic respiration), pyruvate is converted into ethanol and CO₂, releasing much less energy; in human muscles during vigorous exercise, pyruvate is converted to lactic acid, causing cramps.
Section D — Long Answer
A29. Diagram: refer NCERT textbook Fig. 6.6 (label mouth, oesophagus, stomach, liver, gall bladder, pancreas, small intestine, large intestine, anus) — 2 marks for a correctly labelled diagram. (i) Bile juice (from liver): makes fats alkaline and breaks them into tiny droplets (emulsification) — increases the surface area for enzyme action; contains no digestive enzyme itself. (ii) Pancreatic juice: contains trypsin (digests proteins) and lipase (digests emulsified fats). (iii) Intestinal juice: contains enzymes that finally convert proteins to amino acids, complex carbohydrates to glucose, and fats to fatty acids and glycerol.
A30. Diagram: refer NCERT textbook Fig. 6.10 (label right/left atrium, right/left ventricle, septum, vena cava, aorta, pulmonary artery/vein) — 2 marks for diagram. Oxygenated blood from lungs → left atrium → left ventricle → pumped to body via aorta. Deoxygenated blood from body → right atrium → right ventricle → pumped to lungs via pulmonary artery. The muscular septum between the two sides prevents oxygenated and deoxygenated blood from mixing, and valves ensure one-way flow.
A31. Diagram: refer NCERT textbook Fig. 6.13 & 6.14 (label kidney, ureter, urinary bladder, urethra, glomerulus, Bowman’s capsule, tubule) — 2 marks for diagram. Three steps: (i) Filtration — blood is filtered under pressure in the glomerulus into the Bowman's capsule. (ii) Selective re-absorption — useful substances (glucose, amino acids, water) are re-absorbed into blood as filtrate passes along the tubule. (iii) Urine formation & release — remaining filtrate (urea, excess water & salts) becomes urine, passing via the ureter to the urinary bladder, and is released through the urethra.
A32. (i) Raw materials: CO₂ (from air, via stomata) and water (from soil, via roots); chlorophyll and sunlight are also needed. (ii) Site: chloroplasts present in mesophyll cells of green leaves. (iii) Three events: absorption of light energy by chlorophyll; conversion of light energy to chemical energy and splitting of water into H and O; reduction of CO₂ to carbohydrates. (iv) Test: a destarched, variegated (or partly-covered) leaf is kept in sunlight, then tested with iodine solution after boiling in alcohol to remove chlorophyll — only the parts that were green and received sunlight turn blue-black, proving that chlorophyll and sunlight are both necessary for starch (food) formation.
