Concept Notes

Read these carefully before attempting the questions — every idea below is used in the practice sections.

1. Parity — Even and Odd

An even number has 2 as a factor (…, −4, −2, 0, 2, 4, …). An odd number does not. The parity rules are:

odd ± odd = even  ·  even ± even = even  ·  odd ± even = odd. A product is even if any one factor is even; it is odd only when every factor is odd.

Key result: For any four numbers a, b, c, d, all 8 expressions of the form a ± b ± c ± d have the same parity. Reason: switching one sign (say +b to −b) changes the value by 2b, an even number — and two numbers differing by an even number always have the same parity.

An expression like 4m + 2q = 2(2m + q) is always even because 2 is a factor. An expression like x2 + 2 is even only when x is even — so it is not always even.

2. Sums of Consecutive Numbers

n consecutive numbers starting at a:  a, a+1, a+2, … Their sum for 4 terms is 4a + 6; for 5 terms it is 5a + 10 = 5(a + 2) — i.e. 5 × middle number.

Every odd number is a sum of two consecutive numbers: 2k + 1 = k + (k + 1). Many numbers can be written as consecutive sums in several ways, but powers of 2 (2, 4, 8, 16, 32, …) cannot be written as a sum of two or more consecutive natural numbers.

3. Multiples, Factors and Combining Divisibility

Rule 1: If a divides M and a divides N, then a divides M + N and M − N.

Rule 2: If A is divisible by k, then all multiples of A are divisible by k, and A is divisible by all factors of k.

Rule 3: If A is divisible by both k and m, then A is divisible by LCM(k, m). So divisible by 9 and 4 ⇒ divisible by 36 (LCM = 36). But divisible by 6 and 4 only guarantees divisibility by LCM(6, 4) = 12, not 24 — e.g. 12 itself.

Testing a composite number: split it into co-prime factors whose product is the number. Test for 6 = 2 × 3 (co-prime ✓). Test for 24 = 3 × 8 (co-prime ✓), never 4 × 6 (they share the factor 2 ✗).

4. What Remains? — Describing Remainders with Algebra

Numbers leaving remainder r on division by d have the form dk + r. Example: remainder 3 on division by 5 → 5k + 3 (3, 8, 13, 18, …). The same family is also “2 less than a multiple of 5”, i.e. 5k − 2.

When two numbers with known remainders are added or subtracted, the remainders add or subtract too (adjust by the divisor if needed). If x leaves 3 and y leaves 5 on division by 7, then x + y leaves 3 + 5 = 8 → 8 − 7 = 1, and y − x leaves 5 − 3 = 2.

5. Divisibility Shortcuts (and why they work)

Writing a number by place value, e.g. 1000d + 100c + 10b + a, explains every shortcut: all place values except units are multiples of 10.

÷2, ÷5, ÷10: look at the units digit (even / 0 or 5 / 0).  ÷4: last two digits (100 and beyond are multiples of 4).  ÷8: last three digits (1000 onwards are multiples of 8).

÷9: every place value is 1 more than a multiple of 9 (10 = 9+1, 100 = 99+1, …), so a number and its digit sum leave the same remainder on division by 9. Divisible by 9 ⇔ digit sum divisible by 9.  ÷3: same idea — digit sum divisible by 3.

÷11: place values are alternately 1 more (1, 100, 10000, …) and 1 less (10, 1000, …) than multiples of 11. So take the alternating sum of digits starting from the units digit: for 328105 → −3 + 2 − 8 + 1 − 0 + 5 = −3, meaning the number is 3 short of (8 more than) a multiple of 11. If the alternating sum is 0 or a multiple of 11, the number is divisible by 11.

6. Digital Roots

Add the digits of a number repeatedly until a single digit remains — this is its digital root. Example: 489710 → 29 → 11 → 2.

The digital root is the remainder on division by 9 (with digital root 9 meaning remainder 0). Multiples of 9 always have digital root 9. Aryabhata II (c. 950 CE), in the Mahāsiddhānta, describes this method — it was used to check arithmetic calculations.

7. Digits in Disguise — Cryptarithms

In a cryptarithm each letter stands for one digit, each digit is used by at most one letter, and a number never starts with 0.

Strategy: (1) work column by column from the units, tracking carries; (2) bound the size — e.g. in BYE × 6 = RAY the product has 3 digits, forcing B = 1; (3) use parity and divisibility — e.g. in JK × 6 = KKK, the product KKK = K × 111 = K × 3 × 37 must be a multiple of 6; (4) test the few remaining cases.

A54.
Let the number be 100a + 10b + c; its reverse is 100c + 10b + a. Difference = 99a − 99c = 99(a − c) — always a multiple of 99 (hence of 9 and 11). Example: 852 − 258 = 594 = 99 × 6. ✓
