2.1

Chemical Properties of Acids and Bases

(a) Indicators

Acids and bases can be identified using acid–base indicators — dyes (or mixtures of dyes) that change colour depending on whether they are in an acidic or a basic medium.

IndicatorColour in AcidColour in BaseColour in Neutral
Red litmusRed (no change)Turns BlueRed
Blue litmusTurns RedBlue (no change)Blue
PhenolphthaleinColourlessPinkColourless
Methyl orangeRed / PinkYellowOrange

Olfactory indicators are substances whose odour changes in acidic or basic media, e.g. onion and vanilla essence (smell disappears in a basic solution); clove oil.

(b) Reaction of Acids and Bases with Metals
Acid + Metal → Salt + Hydrogen gas
Zn(s) + H2SO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + H2(g)
2NaOH(aq) + Zn → Na2ZnO2(aq) + H2(g)  (sodium zincate)

See Activity 2.3 / Fig. 2.1 in your NCERT textbook for the labelled diagram of the apparatus used to react zinc granules with dilute sulphuric acid and test the hydrogen gas evolved (soap-bubble + burning candle test — the gas burns with a "pop" sound).

⚠ Exam Tip

Note the exceptions: acids do not react the same way with all metals (e.g. reaction with sodium/potassium is too violent to demonstrate this way). Bases react with only some metals (like Zn, Al) — not all.

(c) Reaction with Metal Carbonates and Metal Hydrogencarbonates
Metal carbonate / Metal hydrogencarbonate + Acid → Salt + Carbon dioxide + Water
Na2CO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)
NaHCO3(s) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)

The CO2 gas evolved is passed through lime water, which turns milky:   Ca(OH)2(aq) + CO2(g) → CaCO3(s) + H2O(l). On passing excess CO2, the milkiness disappears because soluble calcium hydrogencarbonate is formed:   CaCO3 + H2O + CO2 → Ca(HCO3)2.

(d) & (e) Reaction with Oxides
  • Metallic oxides + acid → salt + water — metallic oxides are therefore called basic oxides. e.g. CuO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CuCl2(aq) + H2O(l)
  • Non-metallic oxide + base → salt + water — non-metallic oxides are therefore acidic in nature. e.g. CO2(g) + Ca(OH)2(aq) → CaCO3(s) + H2O(l)
2.2

What Do All Acids and Bases Have in Common?

All acids generate H+(aq) ions in aqueous solution, which is responsible for their acidic properties. Because hydrogen ions cannot exist alone, they always combine with a water molecule to form a hydronium ion, H3O+:

HCl + H2O → H3O+ + Cl–   H+ + H2O → H3O+

All bases generate OH–(aq) ions in water. Bases that are soluble in water are specifically called alkalis.

NaOH(s) →Water Na+(aq) + OH–(aq)   KOH(s) →Water K+(aq) + OH–(aq)
✎ Why compounds like glucose and alcohol are NOT acids

Glucose and alcohol also contain hydrogen, but on testing (using a circuit with a bulb), their aqueous solutions do not conduct electricity, since they do not ionise to release H+ ions in solution. Hence, mere presence of hydrogen is not enough — a compound is acidic only if it produces H+(aq) ions in water.

Dry HCl gas does not change the colour of dry litmus paper — because H+ ions form only in the presence of water; separation of H+ from HCl molecules cannot occur in the absence of water. This is why concentrated acids/bases should be handled with care and always diluted by adding the acid slowly to water (with constant stirring), never water to acid — the process of dissolving an acid or base in water is highly exothermic, and adding water to a concentrated acid can cause the mixture to splash out and cause burns.

Neutralisation Reaction

Since all acids give H+(aq) and all bases give OH–(aq), a neutralisation reaction can be represented as:

Acid + Base → Salt + Water
H+(aq) + OH–(aq) → H2O(l)

Mixing an acid or base with water decreases the concentration of ions (H3O+/OH–) per unit volume — this process is called dilution.

2.3

How Strong Are Acid or Base Solutions?

The strength of an acid or base solution can be measured using a universal indicator (a mixture of several indicators) which shows different colours at different H+ ion concentrations. The pH scale (p = "potenz", German for power) measures hydrogen ion concentration, ranging from 0 (very acidic) to 14 (very alkaline). Higher the H3O+ concentration, lower is the pH value.

Fig. 1 — The pH scale, with approximate pH values of some common substances.
✎ Key pH facts
  • pH of a neutral solution = 7. pH < 7 → acidic; pH > 7 → basic.
  • Strong acids give more H+ ions than weak acids of the same concentration (and vice-versa for bases).
  • Our body works within a narrow pH range of 7.0 to 7.8.
Importance of pH in Everyday Life
SituationExplanation
Living organismsSurvive only in a narrow pH range; when the pH of rain water is below 5.6, it is called acid rain. When acid rain flows into rivers, it lowers the pH of river water, making survival of aquatic life difficult.
Digestive systemOur stomach produces hydrochloric acid, which helps digest food without harming the stomach. During indigestion, the stomach produces too much acid causing pain; antacids (mild bases, e.g. milk of magnesia) are used to neutralise the excess acid.
Tooth decayStarts when the pH of the mouth falls below 5.5 — bacteria present in the mouth produce acids by degradation of sugar and food particles. Brushing regularly (using tooth pastes, which are generally basic) helps neutralise the excess acid and prevent tooth decay.
Plants and animalsSelf-defence through "chemical warfare" — e.g. bee-sting/ant-sting leaves an acid (methanoic acid) causing pain; rubbing baking soda (a mild base) gives relief. Nettle plant stings inject methanoic acid too, and can be relieved with the leaf of the dock plant.
2.4

More About Salts

2.4.1 Family of Salts

Salts having the same positive (cation) or negative (anion) radical are said to belong to a family. e.g. NaCl and Na2SO4 belong to the family of sodium salts; NaCl and KCl belong to the family of chloride salts.

Common salt (NaCl) is a raw material for many chemicals of daily use — sodium hydroxide, baking soda, washing soda, bleaching powder.

2.4.4 Are the Crystals of Salts Really Dry? (Water of Crystallisation)

Water of crystallisation is the fixed number of water molecules present in one formula unit of a salt. On heating, this water is removed, and the salt changes colour (e.g. blue copper sulphate crystals turn white on heating; on adding a few drops of water, the blue colour is restored).

SaltFormula (with water of crystallisation)
Copper sulphate (hydrated)CuSO4·5H2O
GypsumCaSO4·2H2O
Washing sodaNa2CO3·10H2O

On heating gypsum at 373 K, it loses water molecules and becomes calcium sulphate hemihydrate, CaSO4·½H2O, called Plaster of Paris — a white powder that, on mixing with water, changes back to gypsum, forming a hard solid mass. Used by doctors to support fractured bones, and for making toys, decoration materials.

CaSO4·½H2O + 1½H2O → CaSO4·2H2O
✅ Quick Revision — What You Must Remember
  • Acid + Metal → Salt + H2;   Base + (reactive) Metal → Salt + H2;   Metal carbonate/bicarbonate + Acid → Salt + CO2 + H2O
  • Acids give H+(aq)/H3O+; Bases give OH–(aq). Neutralisation: Acid + Base → Salt + Water.
  • pH scale: 0–14; <7 acidic, =7 neutral, >7 basic. Always add acid to water, never water to acid.
  • Chemicals from common salt: NaOH, bleaching powder (CaOCl2), baking soda (NaHCO3), washing soda (Na2CO3·10H2O), Plaster of Paris (CaSO4·½H2O).
