Concept Notes

3.1 A Torchlight

  • A torchlight (torch/flashlight) has a lamp and a switch. Sliding the switch makes the lamp glow in one position and not in the other.
  • Opening a torchlight shows two or more electric cells inside it.
  • Caution: Never perform experiments with the mains power supply at home or school — even portable generators can be dangerous. For experiments, use only cells or batteries (as in torches, wall clocks, radios, remotes).

3.2.1 Electric Cell

  • An electric cell is a portable source of electrical energy.
  • Every electric cell has two terminals: the positive terminal (+ve) and the negative terminal (–ve).
  • The small protruding metal cap on the cell is the positive terminal; the flat metal disc on the other side is the negative terminal.

3.2.2 Battery

  • A combination of two or more electric cells is called a battery.
  • In a working battery, the positive terminal of one cell is connected to the negative terminal of the next cell.
  • If a cell is placed the wrong way round (reversed), the torch lamp will not glow — the order/direction of cells matters.
  • Connecting more than one cell provides energy to the circuit for a longer time and/or more energy.
  • Fascinating fact: the word ‘battery’ is also loosely used even for a single cell (e.g., a mobile phone ‘battery’ is really a single cell).

3.2.3 Electric Lamp — Incandescent Lamp and LED

  • Incandescent lamp: has a thin wire in the middle of the glass bulb called the filament. The filament is attached to two thick support wires, which form its two terminals (one joins the metal case at the base, the other joins the metal tip at the centre of the base). These two terminals do not touch each other.
  • When current flows through the filament, it gets hot and glows, producing light. If the filament breaks, the lamp is said to have ‘fused’ and no longer glows, because the broken filament stops the flow of current.
  • With an incandescent lamp, it does NOT matter which of its terminals is joined to the positive or the negative terminal of the cell — it glows either way, as long as the circuit is complete.
  • LED (Light Emitting Diode): unlike an incandescent lamp, an LED has no filament. It has two terminals of different wire lengths — the longer wire is the positive terminal and the shorter wire is the negative terminal.
  • Current can pass through an LED in ONE direction only. It glows only when its positive terminal (longer wire) is connected to the positive terminal of the battery, and its negative terminal (shorter wire) is connected to the negative terminal of the battery. Connected the wrong way round, it will not glow at all.

3.2.5 The Electrical Circuit

  • An electrical circuit is a complete, unbroken path through which electric current can flow.
  • The lamp glows only when one terminal of the lamp is connected to one terminal of the cell, and the other terminal of the lamp is connected to the other terminal of the cell — forming a closed loop.
  • By convention, the direction of electric current in a circuit is taken to be from the positive terminal to the negative terminal of the cell.

3.2.6 Electric Switch

  • A switch is a simple device that either completes or breaks a circuit; it can be placed anywhere in a circuit.
  • ON position: the circuit is closed (the gap is bridged), current flows, and the lamp glows.
  • OFF position: the circuit is open (there is an air gap between the switch's terminals), current cannot flow, and the lamp does not glow.
  • A simple switch can be made from two drawing pins, a safety pin (or paperclip) and a piece of cardboard — the rotating safety pin bridges the two drawing pins to close the circuit.

3.3 Circuit Diagrams — Standard Symbols

  • A circuit diagram is a representation of an electrical circuit using standard symbols instead of drawing every component realistically.
  • Electric cell: a long line (positive terminal) and a short line (negative terminal), side by side.
  • Battery: two or more cell symbols placed next to each other.
  • Electric lamp: a circle with a cross (X) inside it.
  • LED: a triangle pointing in the direction current can flow, with two small arrows near the tip showing that it emits light.
  • Switch (ON): a continuous line joining two small circles (closed gap).
  • Switch (OFF): a broken/tilted line between two small circles (open gap).
  • Wire: a plain straight or curved line.
  • International bodies such as the IEC, ANSI and IEEE standardise these symbols so people everywhere can read circuit diagrams the same way.

3.4 Electrical Conductors and Insulators

  • Materials through which electric current can flow easily are called good conductors (or conductors of electricity). Example: metals.
  • Materials through which current cannot flow are called insulators (or poor conductors of electricity). Example: plastic, rubber, wood, glass, ceramics.
  • Metals are conductors, which is why they are used to make electrical wires.
  • Among metals, silver, copper and gold are the best conductors of electricity. However, copper is mainly used for wires because it is much cheaper and more abundant than silver or gold.
  • Conductors and insulators are both essential: wires, switch contacts, plug pins and sockets are made of conductors; insulators like rubber, plastic and ceramic are used to cover wires, plug tops and switch handles to protect people from electric shocks.
  • Caution: Our body is also a conductor of electricity, so current passing through it can cause severe injury or even death. Always handle electrical appliances with care; never touch switches or plugs with wet hands, and never use electrical devices in wet areas or with damaged insulation.
  • Electricity from a cell/battery is Direct Current (DC) — it flows in one direction only and usually powers small, portable devices. Electricity from power plants that reaches our wall sockets is Alternating Current (AC), which periodically reverses direction and can run larger appliances.

Section D — Short Answer Questions

  1. The ‘+’ and –’ signs have worn off a battery, so its terminals cannot be identified by sight. Suggest a method, using an LED, to identify which terminal of the battery is positive.Since an incandescent lamp glows regardless of polarity, it cannot help here — but an LED can, because it only conducts one way. Connect one terminal of the battery to the LED's longer wire and the other terminal to its shorter wire. If the LED glows, the terminal touching the longer wire is the battery's positive terminal (and the other is negative). If it does not glow, swap the two connections — it will glow this time, again showing the terminal touching the longer wire is positive.
  2. A torch stops working. On opening it, a student finds that one of its two cells has been placed the wrong way round (reversed). Explain why the torch does not work in this condition.For a battery to work correctly, the positive terminal of one cell must be connected to the negative terminal of the next cell. If one cell is reversed, this order is broken, so the cells do not add up correctly and the circuit does not get proper current — the lamp does not glow.
  3. Why can a switch be placed at any point in a simple circuit and still work correctly?In a simple (single-loop) circuit, all components are connected one after another in the same path. A break at any point — including at the switch — stops current everywhere in that loop. So it does not matter where along the loop the switch is placed; opening it anywhere breaks the whole circuit.
  4. Why are electrical wires usually covered with a layer of plastic or rubber?Plastic and rubber are insulators — they do not allow electric current to pass through them. Covering the metal wire (a conductor) with them stops the current from escaping outward and protects people from getting an electric shock if they touch the wire.
  5. State one reason why copper, and not silver, is generally used for making household electrical wiring, even though silver is a better conductor.Copper is much cheaper and far more abundantly available than silver, making it more practical and economical for large-scale use in wiring, even though silver conducts electricity slightly better.
  6. A student forgot to strip (remove the insulating covering from) the ends of the connecting wires before setting up a circuit. If the cell and the lamp are both in perfect working order, will the lamp glow? Give a reason.No, the lamp will not glow. The insulating covering does not allow current to pass through it, so even though the wires are physically touching the terminals, no current can actually flow, and the circuit remains incomplete in effect.
  7. Explain briefly how a simple ON/OFF switch made from two drawing pins, a safety pin and a piece of cardboard works.One drawing pin holds the safety pin so it can rotate freely; the second drawing pin is fixed where the free end of the safety pin can reach it. When the safety pin is rotated to touch both drawing pins, it bridges the gap and closes the circuit (ON, current flows). When it is rotated away so it touches only one drawing pin, there is a gap in the path, so the circuit is open (OFF, no current flows).
  8. Give any two safety precautions that should be followed while handling electrical appliances or wires at home.Any two of: never touch switches or plugs with wet hands; never perform experiments with the mains power supply; never use electrical devices in wet areas; never handle equipment with damaged insulation or broken plugs.

Section F — Application-Based / HOTS Questions

  1. Two identical lamps, L1 and L2, are connected one after another (in a single loop, i.e. in series) along with a battery and two switches, S1 and S2, which are also part of the very same loop. If S2 is in the ON position but S1 is in the OFF position, will L1, L2, both, or neither glow? Justify your answer.Neither lamp will glow. Since all components are in a single series loop, S1 being OFF creates a gap in the only available path, so no current can flow anywhere in the loop — regardless of S2's position.
  2. In the same circuit as above, if S1 is ON but S2 is OFF, which lamp(s) will glow?Neither lamp will glow, for the same reason — a single loop is broken by S2 being OFF, so current cannot flow through any part of the loop, including L1.
  3. In the same circuit, if both S1 and S2 are in the ON position, which lamp(s) will glow?Both L1 and L2 will glow, since the loop is now completely closed and current can flow through the entire path, including both lamps.
  4. In the same circuit, if both S1 and S2 are in the OFF position, which lamp(s) will glow?Neither lamp will glow, as there are now two gaps in the loop and no current can flow at all.
  5. In the circuit above (L1 and L2 in series, both switches ON), the filament of L1 suddenly breaks. Will L2 continue to glow? Justify your answer.No, L2 will also stop glowing. Since L1 and L2 are connected in series (a single loop), a broken filament in L1 creates a gap in the only path available for current, so current stops flowing through the entire loop — including L2.
  6. Vidyut sets up a circuit with a cell, a lamp and a switch, all connected in a loop, and closes the switch — but the lamp still does not glow. List at least four different possible reasons for this, and briefly state how you would check each one.Possible reasons (any four): (1) The cell may be dead/discharged — test it with a known working lamp or LED. (2) One of the wire connections may be loose or not touching a terminal properly — check and tighten each connection. (3) The lamp's filament may have fused (broken) — inspect the filament or try a different lamp. (4) The switch may not be closing properly, i.e., its moving part may not be touching both contacts — examine and adjust the switch. (5) One of the connecting wires may be internally broken — test each wire by swapping it with a wire known to be working.
  7. Using an LED that needs two cells connected in series to glow, Tanya sets up a circuit with a battery of two cells but connects only the LED's shorter wire to the battery's positive terminal wire and its longer wire to the battery's negative terminal wire. Will the LED glow? If not, how should the connections be corrected?No, the LED will not glow, because it is connected the wrong way round — current can pass through an LED in only one direction. To correct this, the LED's longer wire (its positive terminal) must be connected to the battery's positive terminal, and its shorter wire (its negative terminal) must be connected to the battery's negative terminal.
  8. Riya wants to light an LED using a single electric cell (1.5 V). She connects it correctly — longer wire to positive, shorter wire to negative — but the LED does not glow at all. However, when she uses two such cells connected in series (as a battery), the very same LED glows brightly. Explain why this might happen, even though the connections were correct both times.Even when connected with the correct polarity, an LED needs a certain minimum voltage to conduct and glow. A single cell may not supply enough voltage for this particular LED, so it does not light up. Two cells in series provide a higher combined voltage, which is enough to make the LED glow.
  9. Suppose the power supply to your area is disrupted for two full days. Based on what you have learnt about electricity in this chapter and in daily life, list at least four everyday activities or devices in your home that would be affected, and explain briefly why each depends on electricity.Any four reasonable examples, e.g.: (1) Lighting — lamps/tube-lights run on electricity from the wall socket (AC) and would not work. (2) Refrigeration — the fridge needs a continuous electric supply to keep food cold, so food may spoil. (3) Charging devices — mobile phones, laptops etc. cannot be charged without mains supply (though a charged battery may last a while). (4) Fans/coolers — would stop working, making the heat harder to bear. (5) Water pumps — many homes use electric pumps to draw water, which would stop working. Section G — Long Answer Questions

  1. Explain the construction and working of an incandescent lamp. Then explain how an LED is different from it in terms of construction, terminals, and the effect of reversing its connections in a circuit.Incandescent lamp: A thin wire called the filament sits inside a glass bulb and is attached to two thicker support wires forming its two terminals — one connects to the metal case at the lamp's base, the other to the metal tip at the centre of the base; these two terminals do not touch each other. When the lamp is connected in a complete circuit, current flows through the filament, heating it until it glows and produces light. It does not matter which terminal of the lamp is connected to the positive or negative terminal of the cell — it will glow either way, as long as the circuit is complete. LED: An LED has no filament at all. It has two terminals formed by two wires of different lengths — the longer wire is its positive terminal and the shorter wire is its negative terminal. Current can flow through an LED in one direction only. It will glow only if its longer (positive) wire is connected to the positive terminal of the battery and its shorter (negative) wire is connected to the negative terminal of the battery. If the connections are reversed, the LED simply will not glow — unlike an incandescent lamp, which is unaffected by such a reversal.
  2. Describe, step by step, how you would construct a simple switch using two drawing pins, a safety pin (or paperclip) and a piece of cardboard, and explain how it behaves in the ON and OFF positions when connected in a circuit with a cell and a lamp.Construction: Take two drawing pins, a safety pin, two wires and a small piece of cardboard. Insert one drawing pin through the ring of the safety pin and fix it to the cardboard so the safety pin can rotate freely. Fix the second drawing pin to the cardboard at a spot where the free end of the safety pin can reach and touch it. Connect one wire to each drawing pin — the switch is now ready. Working: When this switch is connected along with a cell and a lamp in a loop, rotating the safety pin so its free end touches the second drawing pin closes the gap and completes the circuit (ON position) — current flows and the lamp glows. Rotating the safety pin away, so it no longer touches the second pin, creates a gap in the circuit (OFF position) — current cannot flow, and the lamp does not glow.
  3. Explain why both conductors and insulators are essential in everyday electrical wiring. Support your answer with one everyday example that uses both together.Conductors, such as copper, allow electric current to flow easily and are used to make the actual current-carrying parts of a circuit — wires, plug pins, switch contacts and sockets. Without conductors, current could not reach our appliances at all. Insulators, such as plastic, rubber and ceramics, do not allow current to pass through them; they are used to cover conductors so that current stays confined to the intended path and does not pass through to anything (or anyone) touching the wire from outside, preventing electric shocks. Everyday example: An ordinary electrical wire has a copper wire (conductor) running through its centre to carry the current, covered on the outside by a plastic or rubber sheath (insulator) that protects anyone who touches the wire from getting a shock.
