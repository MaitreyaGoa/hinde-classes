Concept Notes — Read Before You Solve

1. Square Numbers (Perfect Squares)

A number obtained by multiplying a number by itself is called a square number: n × n = n2. Squares of natural numbers (1, 4, 9, 16, 25, …) are called perfect squares. Squares of fractions and decimals also exist: (3/5)2 = 9/25 and (2.5)2 = 6.25.

Why "square"? The area of a square of side n units is n × n square units — so the number n2 literally counts the unit squares inside a square.

2. Properties of Perfect Squares

  • Units digit: Perfect squares end only in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 or 9. A number ending in 2, 3, 7 or 8 is never a perfect square. (But ending in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, 9 does not guarantee a square — e.g. 26 is not a square.)
  • Zeros: A perfect square can only end with an even number of zeros. (A number ending in exactly 3 zeros has a square ending in 6 zeros.)
  • Parity: Square of an even number is even; square of an odd number is odd.
  • Factors: Only perfect squares have an odd number of factors — because one factor pairs with itself (e.g. 36 = 6 × 6).
  • Between squares: Exactly 2n numbers lie between n2 and (n+1)2.

3. Perfect Squares and Odd Numbers

1 + 3 + 5 + … + (2n − 1) = n2  ·  The nth odd number is (2n − 1)

Every square is a sum of consecutive odd numbers starting from 1. So we can test a perfect square by successively subtracting 1, 3, 5, 7, … — if we reach exactly 0, the number is a perfect square, and the number of steps is its square root. Also, (n+1)2 = n2 + (n+1)th odd number.

4. Perfect Squares and Triangular Numbers

Triangular numbers: 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21, … The sum of two consecutive triangular numbers is always a perfect square: 1 + 3 = 4, 3 + 6 = 9, 6 + 10 = 16, 10 + 15 = 25, …

5. Square Roots

If y = x2, then x is a square root of y. Every perfect square has two integer square roots, one positive and one negative (82 = 64 and (−8)2 = 64). The symbol √ denotes the positive square root.

Finding square roots:

  • Prime factorisation: split the prime factors into two identical groups. 324 = (2 × 3 × 3) × (2 × 3 × 3) = 182, so √324 = 18. If the factors cannot be paired, the number is not a perfect square (e.g. 156 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 13).
  • Successive subtraction of odd numbers (works, but slow for big numbers).
  • Estimation: trap the number between known squares, use the units digit, then narrow down. E.g. 402 < 1936 < 502; last digit 6 ⇒ root ends in 4 or 6; 452 = 2025 > 1936 ⇒ √1936 = 44.

6. Cubic Numbers (Perfect Cubes)

n × n × n = n3 is the cube of n. Perfect cubes: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, … A cube of side 4 contains 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 unit cubes. Cubes of negatives are negative: (−6)3 = −216. Cubes can end in any digit 0–9, and can only end in a number of zeros that is a multiple of 3.

Sum of consecutive odd numbers gives cubes too: 1 = 13,  3+5 = 23,  7+9+11 = 33,  13+15+17+19 = 43 …

7. Cube Roots

If y = x3, then x = ∛y. To test a perfect cube by prime factorisation, check whether the prime factors split into three identical groups (equivalently, every prime appears in triples): 3375 = (3×5) × (3×5) × (3×5) = 153, so ∛3375 = 15. But 500 = 2 × 2 × 5 × 5 × 5 cannot be split — not a cube.

Quick trick for guessing cube roots (of perfect cubes up to 6 digits): the units digit of the cube tells the units digit of the root (1→1, 2→8, 3→7, 4→4, 5→5, 6→6, 7→3, 8→2, 9→9, 0→0); the thousands part tells the tens digit. E.g. ∛12167: ends in 7 ⇒ root ends in 3; 12 lies between 23=8 and 33=27 ⇒ tens digit 2. So ∛12167 = 23.

8. Successive Differences

For perfect squares, the differences 3, 5, 7, 9, … become constant (all 2) at Level 2. For perfect cubes, the differences become constant (all 6) at Level 3.

9. Taxicab Numbers & A Pinch of History

  • 1729, the Hardy–Ramanujan number, is the smallest number expressible as the sum of two cubes in two different ways: 13 + 123 = 93 + 103. Such numbers are called taxicab numbers.
  • The Babylonians compiled the first known lists of perfect squares and cubes on clay tablets (~1700 BCE).
  • Sanskrit terms: varga = square, ghana = cube, varga-varga = fourth power; mula (root of a plant) gave us "square root" — varga-mula and ghana-mula. Brahmagupta (628 CE) used pada: "the pada (root) of a krti (square) is that of which it is a square." Aryabhata (499 CE) defined varga as both the square figure and the square power.

11.

2032 (ends in 2), 2048 (ends in 8) and 1027 (ends in 7) are definitely not perfect squares — no square ends in 2, 3, 7 or 8. 1089 cannot be ruled out this way; in fact 1089 = 332, a perfect square.

1082 and 2922. A square ends in 4 only when the number ends in 2 or 8. (108 ends in 8; 292 ends in 2.) 642 and 362 end in 6.

Check: 108² = 11664, 292² = 85264, 64² = 4096, 36² = 1296.

13.

1262 = 1252 + 125 + 126 = 15625 + 251 = 15876. (Equivalently, add the 126th odd number, 2×126 − 1 = 251.)

14.

(i) 2 × 16 = 32   (ii) 2 × 99 = 198   (iii) 2 × 25 = 50

15.

137 is odd, so 1372 is odd. In general, a number and its square always have the same parity: odd² is odd, even² is even.

16.

17.

Squares, Odd Numbers & Triangular Numbers

19.

20.

64 − 1 = 63; 63 − 3 = 60; 60 − 5 = 55; 55 − 7 = 48; 48 − 9 = 39; 39 − 11 = 28; 28 − 13 = 15; 15 − 15 = 0. Reached 0 in 8 steps ⇒ 64 is a perfect square and √64 = 8.

The 46th odd number is 2 × 46 − 1 = 91. So 462 = 2025 + 91 = 2116.

(i) False — odd × odd × odd = odd (e.g. 3³ = 27). (ii) False — 2³ = 8 and 12³ = 1728 end in 8. (iii) False — the smallest 2-digit cube is 10³ = 1000, already 4 digits. Patterns, Taxicab Numbers & History

41.

Cubes: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, 343.
Level 1: 7, 19, 37, 61, 91, 127.
Level 2: 12, 18, 24, 30, 36.
Level 3: 6, 6, 6, 6. The differences become constant at Level 3, and the constant is 6. (Squares stabilise at Level 2 with constant 2 — cubes need one more level.)

42.

4104 = 23 + 163 (8 + 4096) = 93 + 153 (729 + 3375).   13832 = 23 + 243 (8 + 13824) = 183 + 203 (5832 + 8000).

43.

(i) 673 − 663 = 300763 − 287496 = 13267 — the greatest. (ii) 43³ − 42³ = 5419; (iii) 67² − 66² = 133; (iv) 43² − 42² = 85. Reasoning: consecutive cube gaps grow much faster than square gaps, and gaps grow with size — so the cube difference at the larger number wins.

44.

The Babylonians, around 1700 BCE, on clay tablets. The lists were used to quickly find square roots and cube roots in land measurement, architectural design and other geometric calculations.

45.

varga = square (figure/area/power); ghana = cube; varga-varga = fourth power; varga-mula = square root; ghana-mula = cube root; pada = root (Brahmagupta, 628 CE: "the pada of a krti is that of which it is a square").

46.

Hardy visited Ramanujan in hospital and remarked that his taxi number, 1729, was "rather a dull number." Ramanujan replied instantly that it is very interesting — it is the smallest number expressible as the sum of two cubes in two different ways: 1³ + 12³ = 9³ + 10³ = 1729. Hence it is called the Hardy–Ramanujan number, and such numbers are taxicab numbers.

Challenge Corner (HOTS)

47.

(i) A locker stays open iff it is toggled an odd number of times, i.e. iff its number has an odd number of factors — exactly the perfect squares: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100 (10 lockers). (ii) Touched exactly twice = exactly two factors = prime-numbered lockers. (iii) Passcode: 2-3-5-7-11.

48.

One valid row: 16, 9, 7, 2, 14, 11, 5, 4, 12, 13, 3, 6, 10, 15, 1, 8, 17 (adjacent sums: 25, 16, 9, 16, 25, 16, 9, 16, 25, 16, 9, 16, 25, 16, 9, 25 — all squares). The arrangement is essentially unique: 16 and 17 have only one possible neighbour each (9 and 8 respectively), forcing them to the two ends, and each internal step is then forced — so the only other arrangement is this row reversed.

49.

1, 64, 729. (64 = 8² = 4³; 729 = 27² = 9³.) These are exactly the sixth powers: 1⁶, 2⁶, 3⁶ — a number is both a square and a cube iff it is a perfect sixth power.

50.

(i) √2025 = 45, so 45 saplings per row (45 × 45 grid). (ii) Largest square ≤ 2000 is 44² = 1936, so 2000 − 1936 = 64 saplings left over.

51.

3³ + 4³ + 5³ = 27 + 64 + 125 = 216 = 6³ — a perfect cube with cube root 6. It is not a perfect square: 14² = 196 < 216 < 225 = 15², so 216 falls strictly between consecutive squares.
