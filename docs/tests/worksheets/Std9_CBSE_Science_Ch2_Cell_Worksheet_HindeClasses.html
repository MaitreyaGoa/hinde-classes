Concept Map

A cell is the smallest unit that can carry out all functions of life on its own. Every topic in this chapter builds towards one big idea: structure decides function — the shape and parts of a cell explain what it can do. The chapter has five big ideas: (1) how scientists study cells, (2) the boundary and coverings of a cell, (3) the organelles inside a cell and their jobs, (4) how cells grow and divide, and (5) the Cell Theory that ties it all together.

2.1  Studying Cells — Microscopes, Resolution & Magnification

Why cells need a microscope must-know

The naked human eye can only tell apart two points that are at least about 0.1 mm (100 µm) apart when viewed from the near point (25 cm). This smallest distance the eye can distinguish is called the limit of resolution of the eye. Since most cells are far smaller than 0.1 mm, they cannot be seen without help.

Robert Hooke (1665) was the first to see cells — while looking at a thin slice of cork with a hand-built microscope (about 200–300× magnification), he noticed tiny box-like compartments and named them "cells" (after the small rooms, or "cellulae", in a monastery).

  • Light microscope: uses visible light and glass lenses; found in school labs; can magnify roughly up to a few hundred to about a thousand times; good enough to see most plant and animal cells and their bigger organelles.
  • Electron microscope: uses a beam of electrons instead of light; magnifies far more and can even show structures at the nanometre scale (viruses, ribosomes, membrane layers).

Formulas you must remember

• Total magnification = Eyepiece magnifying power × Objective lens magnifying power

• Estimated real size of a cell = (Diameter of the visible field in µm) ÷ (Number of cells counted along that diameter)

• Unit ladder: 1 mm = 1000 µm and 1 µm = 1000 nm, so 1 mm = 106 nm.

Worked Example: The circular field of view under a microscope has a diameter of 5 mm. 20 onion-peel cells are seen lying end-to-end across this diameter. Both the eyepiece and objective lens have a power of 10×. Find (a) the estimated size of one cell, and (b) the total magnification.

Solution: (a) Diameter in µm = 5 × 1000 = 5000 µm. Size of one cell = 5000 ÷ 20 = 250 µm.   (b) Total magnification = 10 × 10 = 100×.

2.2  Boundaries of the Cell

2.2.1 Cell membrane (plasma membrane) — the universal boundary

Every single cell — plant, animal, fungal or bacterial — is wrapped in a thin, living boundary called the cell membrane. It is selectively permeable: it lets some substances cross while blocking others, which is how the cell controls what enters and leaves it.

📚 Refer to your textbook: Fig. 2.7 "Structure of a cell membrane" — shows the lipid bilayer with water-loving heads outward, water-repelling tails inward, and proteins embedded across it.
  • Made of a lipid bilayer (two layers of fat molecules) with different proteins embedded in it — this is called the fluid-mosaic model (proposed by Singer & Nicolson).
  • "Fluid" — the lipid and protein molecules can slide, flip and rotate sideways within the layer.
  • "Mosaic" — the scattered proteins look like tiles set into the lipid layer; these proteins act as gatekeepers, helping specific substances pass through.
  • Only about 7–10 nanometres thick, yet strong enough to hold the entire cell together.

Diffusion & Osmosis frequently tested

Net movement of any particles from a region of higher concentration to lower concentration. Movement of water only, from a dilute solution to a concentrated solution, through a selectively permeable membrane.
Does not need a membrane.Must occur across a selectively permeable membrane.
📚 Refer to your textbook: Fig. 2.6 "Effect of solutions of different concentrations on a cell" — shows a cell's response in isotonic, hypotonic and hypertonic surroundings.
MediumComparison of solute concentrationEffect on the cell
IsotonicOutside = InsideNo net movement of water; cell size stays the same.
HypotonicOutside < Inside (dilute outside)Water enters the cell → cell swells (an animal cell may even burst; a plant cell becomes firm/turgid because the wall resists bursting).
HypertonicOutside > Inside (concentrated outside)Water leaves the cell → cell shrinks (crenation in an animal cell; plasmolysis in a plant cell).

Classic potato experiment: A potato piece placed in plain water gains weight and swells (water moves in by osmosis because the outside is hypotonic). The same potato placed in a 20% salt/sugar solution loses weight and becomes limp (water moves out because the outside is hypertonic).

2.2.2 Cell wall — the outer covering of plant, fungal & bacterial cells

Plants cannot run away from wind, rain or grazing animals, so their cells need extra support. The cell wall lies outside the cell membrane and is rigid but permeable (water and dissolved minerals can still pass through).

  • In plants, the cell wall is made mainly of cellulose, a carbohydrate; this cellulose acts as roughage/fibre in our diet.
  • Fungal cell walls are mostly made of chitin; bacterial cell walls are made of peptidoglycan — both give rigidity just like cellulose does in plants (an extra fact worth remembering for comparison questions).
  • The cell wall keeps a plant cell's shape/boundary fixed even when it loses water — only the inner cell contents (membrane + cytoplasm) shrink away from the wall. This shrinking of cell contents away from the wall in a concentrated solution is called plasmolysis.
  • Animal cells have no cell wall, so they can change shape easily — useful for movement — but they also shrink or burst more easily than plant cells in extreme solutions.

2.3  Inside the Cell — Organelles & Their Jobs

Nucleus — control centre

📚 Refer to your textbook: Fig. 2.11 "Structure of a nucleus" — shows the double-layered nuclear membrane, nuclear pores, nucleolus and chromatin.
  • Nuclear membrane: double-layered, with pores that let material pass between nucleus and cytoplasm.
  • Nucleolus: dense round body inside the nucleus; makes ribosomal subunits.
  • Chromatin/Chromosomes: thread-like DNA + protein complex; condenses into rod-shaped chromosomes only when a cell is about to divide. Genes are the functional segments of DNA that carry hereditary information.

Ribosomes, ER & Golgi — the assembly line

  • Ribosomes: tiny particles, free in the cytoplasm or attached to ER; the site of protein synthesis.
  • Rough ER (RER): studded with ribosomes; makes and ships proteins.
  • Smooth ER (SER): no ribosomes; makes and stores fats/lipids and hormones.
  • Golgi apparatus: stacks of flattened sacs; the cell's "post office" — modifies, sorts and packages proteins/lipids into vesicles for transport, secretion, or to form lysosomes. First seen by Camillo Golgi (1898).

Lysosomes & Mitochondria

Lysosomes — single membrane-bound sacs full of digestive enzymes; the cell's "clean-up crew". They break down worn-out organelles and unwanted material, and the products can be reused by the cell. (In sperm cells, a lysosome-like structure called the acrosome releases enzymes that help penetrate the egg during fertilisation.)

📚 Refer to your textbook: Fig. 2.14 "Structure of a mitochondrion" — shows the outer membrane, inner membrane, cristae and matrix.

Mitochondria ("powerhouse of the cell") — supply energy through cellular respiration, breaking down glucose to release energy stored in ATP molecules. Each mitochondrion has an outer membrane (smooth) and an inner membrane folded into finger-like cristae that increase surface area for reactions. Mitochondria carry their own DNA and ribosomes, which is why scientists believe they evolved from free-living bacteria engulfed by an ancestral cell long ago (the endosymbiotic theory).

Plastids & Vacuoles — special to plant cells

📚 Refer to your textbook: Fig. 2.15 "Structure of a chloroplast" — shows the outer/inner membrane, stroma and the disc-shaped membranes that contain chlorophyll.
Type of plastidColour/PigmentRole
ChloroplastGreen (chlorophyll)Traps sunlight for photosynthesis; food made here is stored in the stroma along with starch grains.
ChromoplastYellow/orange/red pigmentsGives colour to flowers & fruits to attract pollinators and seed-dispersers.
LeucoplastColourlessStores food — starch (e.g. potato), oils or proteins.

Vacuole: a mature plant cell usually has one large central vacuole full of cell sap (water, minerals, sugars, wastes), surrounded by a selectively permeable membrane. It keeps the cell firm (turgid) by storing water under pressure; a plant wilts when its vacuoles lose water. Animal cells may have small, temporary vacuoles.

2.4  Cell Growth & Division

A cell can only grow up to a certain size (a small cell has more surface area relative to its volume, which makes exchange of materials with its surroundings efficient). Once it reaches this limit, it divides to form new cells instead of continuing to swell — this is why growth in living things happens mainly through cell division, not just bigger individual cells.

📚 Refer to your textbook: Fig. 2.18 "Mitosis" (1 parent cell → 2 identical daughter cells) and Fig. 2.19 "Meiosis" (1 parent cell → 4 daughter cells with half the chromosome number).
FeatureMitosisMeiosis
Number of divisionsOneTwo, one after another
Daughter cells produced2, genetically identical to parent4, each genetically different
Chromosome number in daughter cellsSame as parent cellHalf that of the parent cell
Where it occursAlmost all body cellsOnly in reproductive organs (testes/ovaries in animals; anthers/ovaries in plants)
Biological roleGrowth, repair, replacement, asexual reproductionFormation of gametes; creates genetic variation

During fertilisation, a sperm and an egg (each with half the usual chromosome number) fuse, restoring the full chromosome number in the new individual — which is why meiosis must halve the number in the first place.

When division goes wrong

  • Normal cells stop dividing once they touch their neighbours — a control called contact inhibition. Cancer cells lose this control and keep dividing uncontrollably, forming tumours (benign or malignant); malignant tumours can invade nearby tissue and spread (metastasise) to other body parts.
  • Errors in meiosis can cause genetic disorders, developmental problems, or reduced fertility.
  • Programmed Cell Death (PCD), or apoptosis, is a controlled, genetically regulated process by which the body removes cells it no longer needs — for example, it removes the webbing between an embryo's fingers so we are born with separate digits.

2.5  The Cell Theory

Three statements, built up by three scientists over about two decades, together form the Cell Theory — the unifying principle of all biology:

  1. All living organisms are made up of one or more cells. (Schleiden, 1838 — plants; Schwann, 1839 — animals)
  2. The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.
  3. All cells arise only from pre-existing cells. (Rudolf Virchow, 1855)

Extension facts: Gottlieb Haberlandt (1902) proposed totipotency — that a single living plant cell can regrow into a whole new plant — laying the foundation of plant tissue-culture technology. In 2010, J. Craig Venter's team built a synthetic copy of a bacterium's DNA and successfully used it to "boot up" an emptied bacterial cell, showing that DNA alone directs a cell's structure and behaviour (though the rest of the cell was not built from scratch).

Section C — VSA Answers 
Q21. Osmosis is the movement of water molecules from a region of dilute (higher water, lower solute) solution to a region of concentrated (lower water, higher solute) solution through a selectively permeable membrane. Difference: diffusion can move any particle and does not need a membrane, while osmosis is specifically the movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane.
Q22. Lysosomes contain powerful digestive enzymes that can break down worn-out cell parts, unwanted proteins, carbohydrates and fats. If the lysosome membrane ruptures, these enzymes can even digest the entire cell — which is why they are nicknamed "suicide bags", while their normal job of tidying the cell earns them the name "clean-up system".
Q23. (i) RER has ribosomes attached to its surface and looks rough; SER has no ribosomes and looks smooth. (ii) RER is mainly involved in protein synthesis and secretion; SER is mainly involved in the synthesis and storage of lipids/fats and hormones.
Q24. (i) Plants cannot move away from environmental stresses like wind and rain, so the rigid wall gives mechanical support and helps them stay upright. (ii) The wall helps leaves and flowers keep their shape, and (along with the selectively permeable cell membrane) allows water and dissolved minerals to be absorbed from the soil by roots.
Q25. The nucleus contains the chromosomes/DNA, which carry all the coded genetic instructions the cell needs to build proteins and regulate every activity — so it directs and controls the working of the whole cell, earning it the name "control centre".
Q26. In a hypotonic solution, water enters both cells by osmosis. The animal cell will swell and may even burst because it has no cell wall to resist the pressure. The plant cell will also take in water and swell, but its rigid cellulose wall resists over-expansion, so it simply becomes firm/turgid rather than bursting.
Q27. A chloroplast contains the green pigment chlorophyll and carries out photosynthesis to make food. A chromoplast contains yellow, orange or red pigments (not chlorophyll) and gives bright colour to flowers/fruits to attract pollinators and animals that help disperse seeds; it does not carry out photosynthesis.
Q28. Schleiden (1838): showed that all plants are made of cells. Schwann (1839): showed that all animals are made of cells. Virchow (1855): added that all new cells arise only from pre-existing cells, completing the Cell Theory.
Section D — SA Answers 
Q29. The cell membrane is made of a lipid bilayer — two layers of fat molecules with their water-attracting heads facing outward (toward water on both sides) and their water-repelling tails facing inward — with various proteins embedded in this layer. Key features: (i) it is fluid — the lipid and protein molecules can move sideways, flip and rotate within the layer; (ii) it is a mosaic — the scattered proteins look like tiles set into the lipid sheet and act as gatekeepers controlling what crosses the membrane. (Diagram: refer to Fig. 2.7 in your textbook — two rows of circular "heads" with tails pointing toward each other, and proteins embedded through the layer.)
Q30. (a) The piece in Beaker A (plain water) gains weight and swells slightly; the piece in Beaker B (20% salt solution) loses weight and becomes limp/shrunken. (b) The process is osmosis — movement of water across the selectively permeable cell membrane. (c) In Beaker B, the salt solution outside is more concentrated (hypertonic) than the potato cells, so water moves out of the potato cells into the solution, causing the piece to shrink and lose weight.
Q31. (i) Nucleus: prokaryotic cells have no well-defined, membrane-bound nucleus (DNA lies in the nucleoid); eukaryotic cells have a true, membrane-bound nucleus. (ii) Organelles: prokaryotic cells lack membrane-bound organelles; eukaryotic cells have organelles such as mitochondria, ER and Golgi apparatus. (iii) Size: prokaryotic cells are typically 1–10 µm across, while eukaryotic cells are typically 10–100 µm across.
Q32. A mitochondrion is enclosed by two membranes — a smooth, porous outer membrane and a folded inner membrane whose finger-like folds (cristae) increase the surface area available for chemical reactions. Inside is the matrix, which along with the cristae is where glucose and other molecules are broken down during cellular respiration to release energy, stored as ATP — which is why mitochondria are called the "powerhouse of the cell". (For the labelled diagram, refer to Fig. 2.14 in your textbook: outer membrane, inner membrane, intermembrane space, cristae.)
Q33. (i) Number of divisions: mitosis involves one division; meiosis involves two successive divisions. (ii) Daughter cells: mitosis gives 2 genetically identical daughter cells; meiosis gives 4 daughter cells, each genetically different from the others and from the parent. (iii) Chromosome number: mitosis keeps the chromosome number the same as the parent; meiosis halves it. (Additionally, mitosis occurs in ordinary body cells for growth/repair, while meiosis occurs only in reproductive organs to form gametes.)
Q34. As a cell grows bigger, its volume increases faster than its surface area (volume grows with the cube of size while surface area grows only with the square). Beyond a certain size, the cell's surface is not large enough to efficiently bring in nutrients/oxygen and remove wastes for the volume of cytoplasm it must serve — so instead of growing indefinitely, the cell divides. The same logic explains why cells keep many small mitochondria rather than one giant one: many small mitochondria together provide far more total membrane surface area (more folded cristae surface) for energy-releasing reactions than a single large mitochondrion of the same total volume could provide.
Section E — Case-Based Answers 
Q35. (1) She was testing whether the concentration of the surrounding solution affects the movement of water into or out of potato cells (i.e., whether osmosis occurs).
(2) Water collected in Cup B's hollow because the salt solution outside the potato cells was hypertonic (more concentrated) than the cell contents, so water moved out of the potato cells, by osmosis, into the hollow and the surrounding solution.
(3) Osmosis (movement of water across the selectively permeable cell membrane).
(4) If Cup A's potato were boiled first, no water would collect in its hollow even with sugar/salt added, because boiling kills the cells and destroys the selective permeability of the cell membrane, so osmosis cannot occur.
Q36. (1) Osmosis (through the selectively permeable membranes of microbial cells).
(2) A high concentration of salt/sugar makes the surrounding medium hypertonic to the cytoplasm of bacteria and fungi present on the fruit, so water is pulled out of these microbial cells by osmosis, dehydrating and killing them or stopping their growth.
(3) The microbial cells lose water and shrink/plasmolyse, which prevents them from multiplying and spoiling the food.

Q37. Diagram: refer to Fig. 2.10(b) "A typical plant cell" in your textbook — showing cell wall, cell membrane, nucleus, vacuole, chloroplast, mitochondria and Golgi body. Six organelles and one function each:
1. Cell wall — gives rigidity and protection.
2. Nucleus — controls all cell activities; stores genetic information.
3. Chloroplast — site of photosynthesis (food-making).
4. Mitochondria — release energy (ATP) through respiration.
5. Vacuole — stores water/minerals/wastes and keeps the cell turgid.
6. Golgi apparatus — packages and ships proteins/lipids.
Q38. Mitosis begins when a cell's chromosomes duplicate; the cell then divides once so that each of the two new daughter cells receives an identical, complete set of chromosomes/DNA. This is how the body grows (adding new cells), replaces dead or damaged cells (e.g. healing a cut), and how many organisms reproduce asexually. The two daughter cells are genetically identical to each other and to the original parent cell. If mitosis occurs in an uncontrolled manner (loss of contact inhibition), two consequences can follow: (i) formation of tumours from the unchecked pile-up of extra cells, which may become cancerous and invade nearby tissue; (ii) daughter cells may end up with an abnormal number of chromosomes, disrupting normal cell function.
Q39. In meiosis, the parent cell (found only in reproductive organs — testes/ovaries in animals, anthers/ovaries in plants) divides twice, one after the other. In the first division, the chromosome number is reduced to half as the cell splits into two; in the second division (similar to mitosis), each of those two cells divides again, giving four daughter cells (gametes) in total, each carrying half the parent's chromosome number. This halving matters because when a sperm and an egg (each with half the number) fuse during fertilisation, the full chromosome number is restored in the offspring. Its significance: it keeps the chromosome number constant across generations and creates genetic variation, which is why offspring resemble but are not identical to their parents. If gametes were instead formed by mitosis, each gamete would carry the full chromosome number; fertilisation would then double the chromosome number in every generation, which would be biologically unsustainable.

Q43. Blood cells are adapted to an isotonic environment (about 0.9% saline). If plain distilled water (which is strongly hypotonic compared to blood cell contents) were injected directly, water would rush into the red blood cells by osmosis, causing them to swell uncontrollably and burst (haemolysis) since they have no cell wall to resist the pressure. Using isotonic 0.9% saline keeps the solute concentration outside equal to that inside the cells, so there is no net movement of water and the cells remain safe and undamaged.
