3.1 Physical Properties of Metals and Non-Metals
PropertyMetalsNon-metals
Physical stateSolid at room temperature (except mercury, which is liquid)Solid or gas (except bromine, a liquid)
LustreShining surface (metallic lustre)Non-lustrous, except iodine
HardnessGenerally hard (alkali metals Li, Na, K are soft and can be cut with a knife)Soft, except diamond (allotrope of carbon) which is the hardest natural substance
MalleabilityCan be beaten into thin sheets (gold and silver most malleable)Not malleable; brittle
DuctilityCan be drawn into wires (gold most ductile)Not ductile
Conduction of heat & electricityGood conductors (silver, copper best); lead, mercury poor conductorsPoor conductors, except graphite (conducts electricity)
SonorousnessProduce sound on striking a hard surface (sonorous)Not sonorous
Melting/Boiling pointGenerally high (gallium, caesium are exceptions with very low melting points)Generally low

Key exceptions to remember (favourite exam trap!)

  • Mercury (Hg) – the only metal that is liquid at room temperature.
  • Gallium and caesium – metals with very low melting points (melt on your palm).
  • Iodine – a non-metal that is lustrous.
  • Graphite – a non-metal (allotrope of carbon) that conducts electricity and is soft/slippery, unlike diamond (also carbon) which is the hardest substance.
  • Alkali metals (Li, Na, K) – soft, low density, low melting point.
3.2 Chemical Properties of Metals

(a) Reaction with Oxygen → Metal Oxides (basic in nature)

Metal + Oxygen → Metal oxide (basic oxide)
  • Sodium and potassium react so vigorously with oxygen that they are stored in kerosene oil to prevent accidental fires.
  • Magnesium burns in air with a dazzling white flame to give MgO.
  • Copper does not burn but the hot metal forms a black coating of copper(II) oxide (CuO).
  • Iron does not burn on heating, but iron filings sprinkled in a flame burn vigorously.
  • Silver and gold do not react with oxygen even at high temperature.
  • Aluminium forms a thin protective oxide layer (Al2O3) that prevents further oxidation – this is why aluminium does not corrode easily.
2Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2MgO(s)   |   2Cu(s) + O2(g) ⟶[heat] 2CuO(s)

Amphoteric oxides: Al2O3 and ZnO react with both acids and bases to form salt and water, so they are called amphoteric oxides (unlike most metal oxides, which are only basic).

Al2O3 + 6HCl → 2AlCl3 + 3H2O   |   Al2O3 + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO2 + H2O

(b) Reaction with Water

Metal + Water → Metal oxide/hydroxide + Hydrogen
MetalReaction with water
K, NaReact violently with cold water; reaction is so exothermic that evolved H2 catches fire
CaReacts with cold water (less violent); metal starts floating because bubbles of H2 stick to its surface
MgDoes not react with cold water; reacts with hot water to form Mg(OH)2 + H2
Al, Zn, FeDo not react with cold or hot water; react with steam to form the metal oxide + H2
Pb, Cu, Ag, AuDo not react with water at all
2Na(s) + 2H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g) + heat
Ca(s) + 2H2O(l) → Ca(OH)2(aq) + H2(g)
2Al(s) + 3H2O(g) → Al2O3(s) + 3H2(g)   |   3Fe(s) + 4H2O(g) → Fe3O4(s) + 4H2(g)

(c) Reaction with Dilute Acids

Metal + Dilute acid → Salt + Hydrogen gas

Metals above hydrogen in the reactivity series displace H2 from dilute acids; metals below hydrogen (Cu, Ag, Au) do not react with dilute HCl or H2SO4.

Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g)   |   Fe(s) + H2SO4(aq) → FeSO4(aq) + H2(g)

Note: Metals react with nitric acid (HNO3) differently – HNO3 is an oxidising agent and normally does not produce H2 (except Mg and Mn with very dilute HNO3).

(d) Reaction with Salt Solutions (Displacement Reaction)

Metal A + Salt solution of B → Salt solution of A + Metal B  (only if A is more reactive than B)
Fe(s) + CuSO4(aq) → FeSO4(aq) + Cu(s)

An iron nail dipped in copper sulphate solution gets coated with reddish-brown copper, and the blue colour of the solution fades as it turns into pale green iron(II) sulphate.

3.3 How Do Metals and Non-Metals React? (Formation of Ionic Compounds)

Elements react to attain a stable, completely-filled outermost shell (noble gas configuration). A metal atom loses electrons to form a positively charged cation; a non-metal atom gains those electrons to form a negatively charged anion. The strong electrostatic force of attraction between the oppositely charged ions is called an ionic (electrovalent) bond, and the compound formed is an ionic compound.

Fig. Mg loses 2 electrons (one to each Cl atom) to form Mg2+; each Cl atom gains 1 electron to form Cl-. The compound MgCl2 formed is held by strong electrostatic (ionic) forces.

Properties of Ionic Compounds

  • Physical nature: Solid and hard due to strong inter-ionic forces of attraction; generally brittle and break into pieces under pressure.
  • Melting & boiling points: High, because a large amount of energy is needed to break the strong inter-ionic attraction.
  • Solubility: Generally soluble in water; insoluble in kerosene, petrol and other non-polar solvents.
  • Conduction of electricity: Conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water (ions become free to move), but do not conduct electricity in the solid state (ions are held in a rigid structure and cannot move).
3.4 Occurrence and Extraction of Metals

Elements/compounds occurring naturally in the earth's crust are called minerals. Minerals from which a metal can be profitably extracted are called ores. The method of extraction depends on the metal's position in the reactivity series.

ReactivityMetalsMethod of extraction
Low reactivityCu, Ag, Hg, AuFound in free state, or easily obtained by heating the ore (e.g. Cu2S + O2 → Cu + SO2, a self-reduction)
Medium reactivityFe, Zn, Pb, CuOre converted to oxide first (roasting/calcination), then reduced with carbon (coke)
High reactivityK, Na, Ca, Mg, AlObtained by electrolytic reduction of the molten ore/chloride

Step 1: Enrichment of Ore

Ores mined from the earth are usually contaminated with impurities called gangue (soil, sand, etc.), which must be removed before extraction.

Step 2: Converting Ore to Metal Oxide (medium-reactivity metals)

Roasting (sulphide ore heated strongly in excess air):  2ZnS(s) + 3O2(g) → 2ZnO(s) + 2SO2(g)
Calcination (carbonate ore heated strongly in limited air):  ZnCO3(s) → ZnO(s) + CO2(g)

Step 3: Reducing Metal Oxide to Metal

ZnO(s) + C(s) → Zn(s) + CO(g)   (reduction using carbon, for medium-reactivity metals)

Highly reactive metal oxides (of Na, Mg, Ca, Al) cannot be reduced by carbon – they are reduced electrolytically, e.g. molten Al2O3 or molten NaCl:

At cathode: Na+ + e- → Na   |   At anode: 2Cl- → Cl2 + 2e-

Displacement by a more reactive metal is also used for some medium-reactivity oxides, e.g. the highly exothermic thermite reaction:

Fig. Thermite reaction: the molten iron produced is used to join railway tracks or cracked machine parts.

Step 4: Refining of Metals – Electrolytic Refining

Metal obtained by reduction is usually impure. In electrolytic refining, the impure metal is the anode, a thin strip of pure metal is the cathode, and a solution of the metal salt is the electrolyte. On passing current, pure metal from the anode dissolves and deposits on the cathode; insoluble impurities settle below the anode as anode mud.

Fig. Impure copper (anode) dissolves into solution; pure copper deposits on the cathode. Insoluble impurities settle below the anode as ‘anode mud’.
3.5 Corrosion and Its Prevention

Corrosion is the slow eating away of a metal surface due to attack by moisture, air or chemicals in the environment (e.g. iron rusting to a brown flaky substance, silver tarnishing black due to H2S, copper gaining a green coat of copper carbonate).

Conditions for rusting of iron: Both air (oxygen) and water must be present together – iron does not rust in dry air or in water free of dissolved air.

Methods of Preventing Corrosion

MethodHow it works
Painting / Oiling / GreasingForms a barrier that keeps out air and moisture
GalvanisationCoating iron/steel with a thin layer of zinc; protects even if the coating is broken (Zn corrodes preferentially)
AlloyingMixing with another metal/non-metal changes properties, e.g. stainless steel (Fe + Cr + Ni) resists rusting
Anodising / Chrome platingElectrochemical build-up of a thicker, protective oxide layer

Alloys

An alloy is a homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or a metal and a non-metal, prepared by melting the primary metal and dissolving the other element(s) in it.

AlloyCompositionKey property / use
Stainless steelFe + Cr + NiHard, does not rust
BrassCu + ZnPoor electrical conductor (unlike pure Cu); used for decorative items
BronzeCu + SnUsed for statues, utensils
SolderPb + SnLow melting point; used to weld electrical wires together
AmalgamMercury + another metalUsed in dentistry, thermometers
Solved Examples
Example 1
Q. Why does calcium start floating when it reacts with water?
A. Ca + 2H2O → Ca(OH)2 + H2. The reaction produces hydrogen gas, and the bubbles of H2 stick to the surface of the metal, making it float.
Example 2
Q. An iron nail is dipped in a blue copper sulphate solution. What changes are observed and why?
A. Iron is more reactive than copper, so it displaces copper from CuSO4: Fe + CuSO4 → FeSO4 + Cu. The nail gets coated with reddish-brown copper, and the blue colour fades because pale green FeSO4 is formed.
Example 3
Q. Why cannot carbon be used to reduce the oxides of Na, Mg, Ca or Al?
A. These metals are more reactive than carbon (higher in the reactivity series) – carbon cannot remove oxygen from their oxides. Hence they must be extracted by electrolytic reduction of the molten ore/chloride.

Answer Key (For Teacher / Self-check)
Section A: 1–(c) Silver; 2–(c) kerosene oil; 3–(c) H2; 4–Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A; 5–Gold (or Silver/Platinum); 6–Solder.
7. They are highly resistant to corrosion (do not react with air/moisture/acids easily) and possess a lasting shine (lustre), making jewellery durable and attractive.
8. 3Fe(s) + 4H2O(g) → Fe3O4(s) + 4H2(g)
9. Oxides that react with both acids and bases to produce salt and water are amphoteric oxides, e.g. Al2O3 and ZnO. Al2O3 + 6HCl → 2AlCl3 + 3H2O (with acid); Al2O3 + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO2 + H2O (with base).
10. Displacement of hydrogen from an acid requires the element to lose electrons and go into solution as a positive ion (be more reactive than hydrogen). Non-metals tend to gain electrons rather than lose them, so they cannot displace H2 from acids; only metals above hydrogen in the reactivity series can.
11. Metals cannot be obtained directly from carbonate/sulphide ores by reduction with carbon efficiently; converting to the oxide first (by calcination for carbonates, roasting for sulphides) gives a form that can be readily reduced by carbon to the metal, since carbon reduces metal oxides but not carbonates/sulphides directly.
12. (i) B is the most reactive metal, since it displaces Fe but nothing displaces B. (ii) Order of decreasing reactivity: B > A > C > D.
13. Anode = impure metal M; Cathode = thin strip of pure metal M; Electrolyte = solution of a soluble salt of M. On passing current, the anode dissolves, pure M deposits on the cathode, and insoluble impurities fall as anode mud. (See labelled diagram in the Concept Notes section.)
14. (a) Corrosion is the gradual deterioration of a metal surface due to reaction with substances in its environment such as moisture, air, and acids. (b) Painting/oiling/greasing – forms a physical barrier that keeps out air and moisture; Galvanising – coating with zinc, which is more reactive and reacts in preference to iron, sacrificially protecting it. (c) Zinc, being more reactive than iron, continues to protect the iron by corroding first (sacrificial protection) even if the surface coating is scratched, until the zinc is used up.
15. (a) Na and K react vigorously (even catch fire) with the oxygen/moisture in air, so are stored under kerosene to prevent contact with air and water. (b) Copper reacts slowly with moist CO2 in air to form green copper carbonate, CuCO3·Cu(OH)2. (c) Aluminium forms a thin, tough layer of Al2O3 on its surface that protects the metal beneath from further reaction, so despite being reactive it does not corrode further and is safe for cookware. (d) Silver reacts with traces of H2S in air to form a black coating of silver sulphide (Ag2S).
